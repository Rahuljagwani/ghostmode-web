"What are your salary expectations?" is one of the few interview questions with real money attached. Say too much and you worry about losing the offer. Say too little and you may live with it for years.

The good news: there's a simple method that works in almost any country, and a few phrases that make the conversation much easier.

## The range method

![A salary bar from the low to the high end of the market, with your range starting around the median and going above it](/blog-images/salary-expectations-interview-answer/range-method.png "Give a range whose bottom you'd happily accept")

1. **Research the market.** The range in the job post (many places now require it), salary sites, recruiters, and people in similar roles. Look for the same role, level, city or remote setup.
2. **Set your floor.** The lowest number you'd accept **without regret**, considering your current pay, other offers and what the new role demands.
3. **Build the range.** Start at your floor or a little above it. A common rule of thumb is a range of roughly 10 to 20% from bottom to top, placed around or above the market middle if your experience fits.
4. **Say it once, calmly.** Then move on to the rest of the package.

**Important:** companies often aim for the bottom of the range you give. That's why the bottom must be a number you're genuinely happy with.

## Example answers

**General (works almost anywhere):**
> "Based on my research for similar roles and my experience with [key skill], I'm looking for a base salary between X and Y. I'm also interested in the full package, so I'm open to discussing it."

**When the job post lists a range:**
> "I saw the posted range of X to Y. Given my experience with [relevant work], I'd expect to be in the upper half of it."

**When it's too early and you don't know the role well yet:**
> "I'd like to understand the responsibilities and level a bit better first. Could you share the range budgeted for this role? I'm sure we can find something that works if it's a good fit."

This is fine to say once. If they ask again, give your range; refusing twice can come across badly.

## India: expected CTC

In India the question is usually "What's your current CTC and expected CTC?"

- **Know the difference between fixed and variable pay.** CTC often includes bonuses, employer PF contributions and other items. Compare **fixed pay** and in-hand salary, not just the headline CTC.
- **Base your expectation on the role and market,** not only on a percentage hike. "I'm looking for 30% more" ties you to your old salary. "For this role, I'm looking for X to Y fixed" ties you to the job.
- **Mention notice period and offers honestly.** A competing offer is useful leverage, but only mention offers you really have.

> "My current CTC is X, of which Y is fixed. For this role, given the scope and the market for [skill], I'm looking for a fixed component between A and B."

## United States

- **Pay transparency laws** in several states (including California, New York, Washington and Colorado) require salary ranges in job posts. Use them.
- **Salary history:** many US states and cities prohibit employers from asking about your current or past pay. You can simply talk about your expectations instead.
- **Total compensation** at tech companies often includes base salary, bonus and equity (RSUs or options). Ask how each part works, including the vesting schedule.

> "I'm targeting a base of X to Y, and I'd like to understand the bonus and equity to look at the total."

## Remote roles paid in dollars (or euros)

Remote roles for international companies are a big opportunity, especially if you're outside the US or Europe. The question becomes: **what does this company pay for this role?**

- **Ask whether pay is location-based.** Some companies pay the same everywhere, many adjust by country or region, and some pay through a contractor platform.
- **Research the company's pay,** not only local salaries: job posts, salary sites and people who work there.
- **Answer in their currency,** and ask about contract type: employee or contractor, who pays taxes, and benefits.

> "Is the salary for this role adjusted by location? Based on what I've seen for similar remote roles, I'm looking for X to Y US dollars a year, depending on the contract and benefits."

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Giving a range whose bottom you'd hate.** You'll often get the bottom.
- **Apologising or over-explaining.** Say the number and stop talking.
- **Basing it only on your current salary.** The new role's market value matters more.
- **Inventing competing offers.** It can backfire badly.
- **Negotiating hard before you have an offer.** Share expectations early; negotiate details once there's an offer in writing.

## After the offer

When the offer arrives, thank them, ask for it in writing, and take a day to review. If it's below your expectation, ask once, specifically: *"I'm excited about the role. Is there flexibility on the base salary to get closer to X?"* Often the answer is yes, or they improve another part of the package.
